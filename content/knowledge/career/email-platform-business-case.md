# The email platform strategy and business case, 2026

In July and August 2026 Matt produced the financial and strategic case for
the company's email-sending platform, building on the V2 sending proof of
concept. The proposal itself is unannounced internal strategy: its figures,
its phasing, and the competitive analysis under it are not public. What
follows is the shape of the work and the reasoning he brought to it.

## What he produced

- A cost analysis of the V2 sending path against the legacy path, modelled
  per thousand emails using actual monthly figures.
- Paired **engineering-edition and executive-summary** business proposals,
  written as two documents for two audiences, delivered to VP-level
  leadership.
- A team product-strategy brainstorm with the product manager, the
  postmaster, and the team's engineers.
- A senior-leadership one-pager package: how the company sends email today,
  the opportunities, a financial summary, and a readiness ladder.

A defined review path was set with his director: him, then his director,
then a senior leader, then wider leadership.

## How the argument is built

The reasoning is the part that generalises. The company already pays to run
a deliverability and compliance capability at a standard most products never
reach, and the case argued that the investment already made should be made
to work harder, phased so that nothing already in production is risked. The
specific proposal and its phasing are internal.

## Competitive discipline

Matt ran a hands-on trial of a competing marketing-automation platform to
get first-party evidence, and wrote the team's rule from it: **never claim
the company's deliverability beats a competitor's.** The defensible claim
is the operations and compliance layer, not inbox-placement bragging
rights.

The competitive reading behind the proposal is internal. The rule he drew
from it is the part that travels.

## What leadership asked for next

The first version was words-heavy, and the next iteration needed to be
consumable for executives: costed options, a competitive analysis, and a
single obvious first use case.

He taught himself business-case writing on the job in 2026, with an AI
assistant as the tutor, rather than through a formal programme.

## Related financial work

- The mail-gateway vendor renewal analysis, in which he validated a volume
  forecast from the warehouse against the vendor's own numbers to within
  about one percent, recommended a specific monthly volume tier from
  modelled volume scenarios, was deferred to by his director on the number,
  had it approved, and saw the contract close at his recommended tier with
  no service disruption during the negotiation.
- A company survey of top projects, where he framed his own as work that
  reduces run-rate and should therefore be prioritised for investment.
- The token-economy framing of the V2 proof of concept: he tracks the API
  spend of his agent-driven proofs of concept and compares it against the
  cost of the planning meetings they replace, arguing the spend was well
  worth it on those terms.

## Why this thread matters

It is the piece of Matt's record where the engineering work stops being the
whole argument. The V2 proof of concept is what made the platform case
credible; the business case is what turns a working prototype into a
funding conversation, and the competitive rule is what keeps it honest.
