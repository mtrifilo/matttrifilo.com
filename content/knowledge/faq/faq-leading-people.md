## How does he handle performance management and growth conversations?

When Matt sets expectations, the conversation begins with a shared understanding of the official roles and responsibilities document for the person's current role, with an eye to the next role they are working towards. With that shared understanding, they look at the team goals and the company goals and discuss which goals the person would be willing to commit to, with measurable outcomes that contribute directly to team goals and company goals. This varies widely with the individual contributor's role, level, and areas of ownership and expertise, as well as how their performance has been trending. The goals are formalized as quarterly goals with measurable KPIs where possible, though the highest priority is the outcomes for the business they are setting goals to deliver: the business outcomes are what everyone is held accountable for when all is said and done, and measurable metrics are helpful as additional data points that tell a more complete story, chosen carefully so that no goal is set around a metric that can be gamed and achieved without any tangible business impact.

For feedback, he has encouraged a culture on his team of raising feedback as close to the moment as possible, for himself and for others on the team, and of giving his own feedback as close to any incident as possible. Especially where a report's performance has declined or needs improvement in general, it is important to him to offer ongoing feedback from a lens of supporting their growth and offering opportunities for them to become a high performer. That works better than raising issues as criticisms with judgement, which can be demotivating and can turn one-on-ones into something a person starts to dread. By framing critical feedback as opportunities to grow and succeed, he can still communicate clear expectations while keeping the conversation focused on growth and improvement and not fixating on how someone is underperforming versus their peers. Good engineers always want to grow and improve, and they can agree on actionable, measurable steps to take to improve performance. Over time they check in on progress without subjective perceptions that could change based on limited observations. The roles and responsibilities document is the source of truth for the core expectations of the "what", and the company values set the expectations of the "how". A formal improvement plan is the last resort, used only when a report cannot meet an agreed goal, and decided on evidence rather than any individual's opinion.

## How does he communicate a change or a reorganization to a team?

During Keap's acquisition into Thryv there was significant change in many aspects of how the team worked, from the systems used to the communication styles used across the organization. Whenever a significant change needs to be communicated, Matt delivers difficult news directly and without sugar-coating, then offers whatever support he can. People react in their own way, and that is theirs to do. He adjusts the delivery to the person, never the message. Where a reaction crosses a professional line, he addresses it with the person as soon as possible, and escalates when necessary. His team has been through a lot of major changes over the years, and they know he will do everything he can to support them through any change and carry any questions or concerns they have to his own leaders, keeping them anonymous if that makes them more comfortable. When a change carries a lot of ambiguity and uncertainty, there have been times when he has asked his Director or VP to address the team and answer questions, which can put people more at ease even when he has little information about the change.

During the first week, he understands that people will likely process the change in waves of grief and acceptance, and more grief at times. During that period he gives people room to process and to vent more than usual. If someone's reaction starts to hurt the team, he takes it to a one-on-one first. He also reminds the team of the free counseling sessions the company offers through its benefits, which are popular and helpful for any employee who needs to talk through a difficult change or transition with a professional counselor.

## How does he build a team that keeps running without him?

This past year Matt handed a time-sensitive and critical migration project to a newer individual contributor, to give them the opportunity to learn a new system and take ownership of a very important project for the team and the business. In the past he would have worked on it himself to make sure it was done on time and without risking other efforts needing to be descoped. After talking with his Product Manager and Tech Lead, all three agreed it would be best to give this engineer the project even if it would take longer, since the initial deadline had been extended, and it would be a great ongoing growth opportunity: learning the new system, delivering a high-profile project, and continuing to own future projects in that area as well. That approach has freed up his time to work on other impactful projects for the organization and the team that he would not have had time for otherwise.

For the past few years he has worked on removing single points of failure on the team: having engineers do more knowledge sharing and documentation of the areas with too much knowledge concentrated in just one or a few individuals, and making sure that critical, aging systems with one maintainer were prioritized for modernization, which cut cloud computing spend while ensuring any engineer can contribute to and triage the system going forward. That has also left the lone maintainer of such a system less on the hook when problems arise, able to take time off more confidently, and free to leave that area to work on new projects. If he were out for a month, the work in flight would not be affected; on-call and the knowledge base run without him. What would slow is new commitments: as the manager he is the bridge between product and engineering priorities, and that negotiation would wait for him. He negotiates to find the best winning scenarios to deliver given the realities of a small team without extra budget to bring on more help. By prioritizing the right outcomes at the right times, the team has always found a way to ship what is most important and impactful for the business goals, with full alignment from Engineering, Product, and senior leadership.
